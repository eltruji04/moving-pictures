# **EPSILON INDI B**
### *The Invisible Companion*

- **Type:** Binary brown dwarf system (T1V + T6V)
- **Distance:** ~12.05 light-years
- **Visual magnitude:** +24.12

At first glance, astronomers thought they had found a single failed star orbiting near a bright neighbor. However, a closer look in 2003 revealed a hidden surprise. That faint point of light was actually two brown dwarfs dancing around each other. Together, they form one of the closest pairs of failed stars to Earth; only the Luhman 16 pair, found in 2013, is known to be closer.

Epsilon Indi B is a system composed of two brown dwarfs, known as Epsilon Indi Ba and Epsilon Indi Bb, which orbit around a common center of mass. These objects occupy an interesting middle ground between giant planets and stars: they are more massive than most planets but lack the conditions required to sustain ordinary hydrogen fusion like stars do.

Despite being relatively close to our Solar System, they are extremely faint in visible light. Most of their detectable emission is concentrated in the infrared spectrum, making them particularly interesting targets for infrared observatories.

Like its stellar companion, Epsilon Indi A, the system has a high proper motion. Its apparent position changes relative to background stars as it travels through the galaxy. Additionally, its two components orbit each other, although this orbital motion is different from the system's overall movement through space.


### *Did you know?*
Brown dwarfs are often called "failed stars" because they never reach the conditions needed to sustain ordinary hydrogen fusion in their cores. However, their atmospheres can have surprisingly similar characteristics to those of giant planets!
